# Speech — Corruption & Everyday Integrity

Let me paint you a picture, somewhere right now, in our country, there's a flood control project that exists only on paper. No cement, no steel, no foundation, just a name on a government contract and someone's signature next to an obscene amount that was never spent the way it should have been.

Today, I want to talk about something that touches every single one of us, no matter our age, our gender,  or our social status. Corruption. And more importantly, what each of us not the Senate, not Malacañang, us the people, can actually do about it.

In a Pulse Asia Survey taken last month, it resulted in Corruption being the number one issue Filipinos want addressed, ahead of price hikes, ahead of job related topics, and even drugs.

I'm sure all of you have heard about the flood control scandal by now. Billions in government funds — taxes paid by everyday Filipinos, meant to protect communities from flooding — became "ghost" projects, inflated costs, and kickbacks. The Philippines is one of the most disaster-prone countries in the world. Every year, we romanticize the idea of the Filipino Resilience — standing firm, weathering the storm, enduring in the face of adversity — when in truth, we should never have had to endure it in the first place. To this day, we still feel the effects: flooded streets, and this week alone, we've been battered by an unpredictable cycle of rain and heat.

Around 9 in 10 Filipinos believe officials colluded to steal these funds — a near-universal loss of trust. The World Bank downgraded the Philippines' 2026 growth forecast partly because of this scandal. The damage isn't abstract — we've felt it. The economy has weakened, jobs have been affected, and buying basic necessities has gotten harder and harder for lower-income Filipinos every month.

But here's the uncomfortable part. We love to point at Manila — at senators, at contractors, at DPWH. And yes, they deserve every bit of scrutiny they're getting. But corruption doesn't survive on its own. It survives because it's normalized in small rooms, small transactions, small silences — including here, including us.


The bribe for a faster permit, an exam shortcut, the concept of fixers, hell even just the unli rice at mang inasal.
 
 these arent billion peso scandals, but they are the exact same, at a smaller scale. If corruption is sustained by millions of small daily choices, then it can also be _broken_ by it. This is where age,gender job titles stop mattering, a lola, a call center agent, a tricycle driver, a wealthy businessman all have equal footing here.


Change starts with us. If we're going to scrutinize government for corruption, we have to hold ourselves to that same standard. So here's what that action looks like:

**One** — refuse the shortcut. The bribe, the grease money. Every refusal is a small act of integrity that costs something now and pays off later.

**Two** — report what you see. The ICI, and ironically enough DPWH itself, opened public anomaly-reporting channels that anyone can use. No rank, no credentials required.

**Three** — vote with memory. If you're of age and eligible to vote, it's your responsibility to vote wisely. Don't accept the bribe money politicians hand out. Remember the corrupt individuals — who was named, who was arrested, who was cleared, and what they actually did.

**Four** — talk about it. Stop treating it as the norm. Every time we normalize it out loud, we make it easier for the next person to do the same.

That flood control project that only exists on paper — it was supposed to protect someone's home. Someone's family. Maybe someone in this room. We can't all sit in the Senate. We can't all audit a government contract. But every one of us can decide, today, that integrity isn't a job title — it's a daily choice. And if enough of us make that choice, that's how a country actually changes.
